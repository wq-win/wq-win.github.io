### Reading B: The Translation on the Wall
When Mira was asked to translate labels for a small local exhibition, she expected the difficult part to be vocabulary. She had studied English for years and brought a thick dictionary to her first meeting. The labels described tools once used by farmers and river workers.

One object was a wooden frame with a name she could not find in any dictionary. An older volunteer offered an explanation, but his first answer was simply, “We used it when the water was low.” Mira wrote down a word that seemed close and moved on. Later, while arranging photographs, she noticed the same frame beside a boat. Her translation made it sound like a tool for gardening. Something was wrong.

She could have left the label as it was. Few visitors would know the difference. Instead, she returned to the volunteer with the photograph. This time she asked him to describe the actions involved rather than supply another name. Using two pencils and a piece of string, he showed her how the frame had held a line in place. Mira’s new label explained the function in a short sentence instead of forcing it into a single familiar word.

At the opening, a visitor read the label and then examined the photograph. “Now I can see how it worked,” she said. Mira still used her dictionary, but it no longer sat alone at the centre of her desk. Beside it were drawings, questions and the volunteer’s contact details. Translation, she had discovered, sometimes began by admitting that a word was not enough.

### Reading C: Darkness as Part of a Habitat
Artificial light changes what a place is like after sunset. A lamp designed to help people find a path may also illuminate nearby plants and water. For animals that are active at night, this can alter the conditions in which they move, feed or avoid predators. The effects vary by species and by the colour, direction and timing of the light.

Researchers studying a riverside path compared insect activity near different lighting arrangements. Their observations suggested that shielding lamps so that light was directed towards the path could reduce some spill into the surrounding habitat. However, a lower count near one lamp did not necessarily mean that fewer insects lived in the area. Insects might have moved, and weather also affected their activity. The researchers therefore combined observations across several nights rather than relying on a single count.

A practical response is not always to remove every lamp. People still need to use paths safely, and different sites serve different purposes. Timers, lower brightness and careful positioning may reduce unnecessary illumination while preserving useful light. These measures should be checked in the actual setting, because a design that works beside one building may not suit an open wetland.

Treating darkness as part of a habitat changes the design question. Instead of asking how much light can be added, planners can ask where light is needed, when it is needed and where it should not go. That approach recognises both human needs and the conditions on which other living things depend.

### Reading D: A Map Is Also a Decision
Digital maps make it easy to collect reports about broken pavements, flooded paths or missing signs. Residents can mark a location and attach a photograph within seconds. For local authorities, the resulting map appears to offer an immediate picture of where work is needed.

But the brightest cluster of reports is not necessarily the area with the greatest need. It may be the area where more people own suitable phones, know about the reporting system or have time to use it. A blank area can mean that nothing is wrong, but it can also mean that a problem has not been reported. Treating absence of reports as absence of need can make existing inequalities harder to see.

This is not a reason to abandon public reporting. Residents often notice problems before an inspection team arrives, and their photographs can help workers prepare. The data become more useful when authorities combine them with scheduled inspections, accessible telephone reporting and contact with groups who are less likely to use the app. Publishing what happened after a report can also show whether the system leads to action rather than simply collecting complaints.

A map is therefore more than a neutral display of dots. Choices about who can contribute, which categories are available and how reports are followed up shape the picture it presents. Good decisions begin by asking not only what the map shows, but also who and what may be missing from it.

### Reading E: Five-Gap Matching
**Starting a Conversation with a Place**
Travelling to an unfamiliar town is more rewarding when it involves more than collecting photographs. [16] A useful starting question might be why the market is held beside the river rather than near the modern shopping centre.

Look for patterns before deciding what they mean. Street names, shop fronts and the way public spaces are used can all provide clues. [17] A single closed shop, for example, cannot tell you that an entire neighbourhood is declining.

When possible, ask people specific, respectful questions. “What used to happen in this square?” is easier to answer than “Tell me everything about your culture.” [18] Someone who has just arrived may describe a place differently from someone who has lived there for decades.

Keep a short record of what you observed and what you were told. [19] This distinction will help you avoid presenting a guess as a fact when you later describe the trip.

You do not need to solve every question during one visit. [20] Returning with a better question may be more valuable than leaving with an apparently complete story.

### Continuation reading for Question 47: The Empty Label
Ellie had helped her mother prepare a box of family photographs for a neighbourhood history day. Her job was to write a short label for each photograph. Most were easy: a wedding, a new shop, a picnic beside the river. One showed a young woman standing in front of a bicycle with a large basket. On the back, someone had written only “June”.

Ellie assumed that June was the woman’s name. She wrote a confident label: “June delivering goods for the family shop.” It sounded like the sort of story visitors would enjoy. Her mother was busy packing, so Ellie did not ask her to check it.

At the exhibition, an older neighbour stopped in front of the picture. “I remember that bicycle,” he said. “But I don’t think June was her name.” Ellie felt the card in her hand grow damp. Other visitors were approaching, and she wished she had left the photograph at home. The neighbour pointed to a small paper sign in the bicycle basket, but the writing was too tiny to read clearly.

Ellie’s mother was speaking to someone at the other end of the room. A magnifying glass lay on the table beside a map. Ellie looked from the neighbour to her neat label. She could pretend that the correction was unimportant, or admit that she had turned a guess into a story.

**Paragraph 1 beginning:** Ellie took the label off the table and reached for the magnifying glass.

**Paragraph 2 beginning:** When her mother returned, Ellie showed her the photograph and the new note.

37. scanning / to scan。scanning the pages为动名词主语；to scan the pages作不定式主语也合语法，本题两者均接受。后面would be为有限谓语，不能再填一个有限谓语。

38.  that。宾语从句many names had been printed…结构完整，that引出发现内容。正式填空常用that；在真实英语中可省，但本题空格要求一词。

39. to check。invite sb to do，居民是check逻辑主语，非谓语不定式宾补。

40. with / to。compare A with/to B在比较义下都可，本题两者均接受；用original作为核对基准。

41. which。逗号后的非限定定从缺主语，指archive，不能用that。

42. a。room为单数可数名词，首次引入一间受控储藏室；carefully起辅音/k/，用a，修饰controlled的副词不改变冠词规则。

43. have used / have been using。since…opened至今；前者强调使用事实，后者突出持续/反复过程，当前文本不能唯一排除，均接受。more people复数。

44. careful。形容词修饰名词interpretation；carefully不直接修饰名词。

45. automatically。副词修饰true所表达的判断关系：可检索不自动使陈述为真；automatic形容词不能直接放在此结构修饰true。

Li Hua

47. 范文：

**Ellie took the label off the table and reached for the magnifying glass.** Beneath it, the small sign became clearer: “Summer Fair”. She showed the neighbour what she could read and admitted that she had guessed the woman’s name and purpose. He remembered a fair near the river, but not the year. Instead of inventing another complete story, Ellie wrote their observations on a fresh card and added two questions about the woman and the date.

**When her mother returned, Ellie showed her the photograph and the new note.** Her mother recognised the woman as a cousin, although she could not remember the exact year either. Together they corrected the name and left the date uncertain. Ellie placed the note beside the photograph, inviting visitors who knew more to help. The label was less confident than her first version, but she was no longer afraid of someone reading it closely.
